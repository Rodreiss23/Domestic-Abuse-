A quiet guide

Abuse is rarely loud.
Learning to see it is the first step.

This page explains what domestic abuse looks like, why the common myths about it are wrong, and where to find real support, for yourself or someone you love.

What it means

Defining domestic violence

Domestic violence, also called intimate partner violence, is any behaviour within an intimate relationship that causes physical, psychological, or sexual harm to those in the relationship, including acts of physical aggression, sexual coercion, psychological abuse, and controlling behaviours.
🏥 WHO World Health Organization (WHO), 2014. Global status report on violence prevention 2014. Geneva: WHO.
The WHO further notes that intimate partner violence is the most common form of violence against women worldwide. It includes a range of behaviours, not just physical assault, and is rooted in power and control. It affects people of all ages, genders, and backgrounds, though women and girls are disproportionately affected.

The scale of it

This is far more common than most people realize

Domestic abuse cuts across every income level, culture, age group, and gender, in Nigeria and worldwide.

1 in 3 Nigerian women aged 15 to 49 have experienced physical violence, most often from a partner.
UN Women
1 in 3 women worldwide experience physical or sexual violence in their lifetime, mostly by an intimate partner.
World Health Organization
Underreported Shame, fear of retaliation, and distrust of the process mean most cases in Nigeria are never formally reported.

The pattern

The cycle of abuse

Abuse often follows a repeating cycle. Understanding it helps explain why leaving is so difficult. The "good times" are part of the pattern, not proof that things are fine.

1⚡

Tension Building

Walking on eggshells. Communication breaks down, minor incidents create dread. The victim feels constant anxiety.

➜
2💥

The Incident

An explosion of abuse: verbal, physical, emotional, or sexual. The abuser acts on the built up tension.

➜
🔄Cycle Repeats
➜
4🌤️

Calm

Things feel normal. The abuse is minimized or denied. Then, inevitably, tension begins to build again.

➜
3🕊️

Reconciliation

Apologies, gifts, promises. "It will never happen again." Hope returns and the victim may withdraw complaints.

Each loop reinforces the abuser's control. The "good phases" are what keep the victim hoping, not evidence of change.

Recognizing it

Abuse takes more forms than physical violence

Most abusive relationships involve a pattern of control, not a single event. These are the most common forms it takes.

Physical

Physical harm

Hitting, restraining, choking, or any use of force meant to hurt, intimidate, or control.

Verbal

Verbal & emotional abuse

Constant criticism, shouting, insults, threats, or blame that erodes someone's sense of self over time.

Financial

Financial control

Restricting access to money, sabotaging work or school, or forcing dependence to limit someone's options.

Digital

Technological monitoring

Tracking a partner's location, reading their messages, or controlling who they can contact.

Coercive

Coercive control

An ongoing pattern of isolation, surveillance, and rules that erode a partner's independence over time.

Sexual

Sexual coercion

Pressuring, guilting, or forcing a partner into any sexual activity they haven't freely agreed to.

Neglect

Neglect

Withholding basic care, attention, or affection as a way to punish, control, or diminish someone.

If you are in danger right now

What to do in an emergency

If you or someone else is in immediate physical danger, act now. You do not need permission or proof.

1📞

Call for help

Dial 112 (national emergency) or 0800 72 73 2255 (toll free). If you cannot speak, leave the line open so they can hear what is happening.

2🚪

Get to safety

Move to a room with an exit (not the kitchen or bathroom). Avoid rooms where weapons are stored. If possible, leave the house and go to a neighbor or public place.

3👤

Tell someone you trust

Call a family member, friend, or neighbor. Let them know where you are and what is happening. You do not have to handle this alone.

4🏥

Go to a hospital or police station

If you are injured, go to the nearest hospital. Ask for a medical report. File a report at the nearest police station or NAPTIP office when you can.

Common misunderstandings

Myths that keep people from getting help

Tap any statement to see why it doesn't hold up.

Leaving is often the most dangerous moment in an abusive relationship. Risk of serious harm rises sharply right after separation. Financial dependence, fear for children, housing, and a realistic fear of retaliation all make leaving far harder than it looks from outside.
Emotional and financial control can be just as damaging as physical violence, and often comes first. Many survivors say the psychological abuse was harder to recover from than any physical injury.
Abuse occurs across every income bracket, education level, religion, and culture. It's simply more visible in some communities than others, and more easily hidden in ones with resources to mask it.
Shame, fear of not being believed, fear of losing custody, and fear of the abuser's response all keep people from reporting. Silence is not evidence that nothing happened.
Many abusers are calm, charming, and well regarded outside the relationship. Abuse is about control, not a loss of temper, which is part of why it is so hard for outsiders to recognize.

A private moment

Could this be happening to you?

Answer honestly. No one else can see this. Nothing is saved or sent. This is not a diagnosis, just a gentle check in.

Do you feel like you're "walking on eggshells" to avoid upsetting your partner?

Does your partner control who you see, where you go, or what you spend?

Do you feel afraid of your partner's reactions?

Has your partner belittled, humiliated, or threatened you, in private or in front of others?

Do you feel isolated from friends, family, or support systems?

After a conflict, does your partner minimize what happened or blame you?

This is not a clinical assessment. If any of this resonates, consider reaching out to a helpline below, even just to talk.

If someone you know is going through this

How to help without taking over

  • 01Believe them, without conditions. The hardest part is often being believed. You don't need proof to offer support.
  • 02Let them set the pace. Avoid ultimatums like "leave or I'm done helping." That pressure can push them back toward the abuser and away from you.
  • 03Help them build a private plan. A safe way to save money, documents, or contacts that their partner can't access or monitor.
  • 04Stay reachable. Isolation is often part of the abuse. Keep the door open even if they're not ready to act yet.
  • 05Point them toward professionals. Hotlines and local advocates are trained for this in ways friends and family, however caring, usually aren't.

Preparing quietly

Building a safety plan

A safety plan is a private, practical set of steps you can prepare in advance. You do not have to act on it today. Having it ready can save your life.

💰

Save money secretly

Keep small amounts of cash in a safe place, or open a bank account your partner does not know about.

📄

Gather important documents

ID cards, birth certificates, marriage certificate, children's school records. Keep copies with someone you trust.

📱

Keep a charged phone

Have emergency numbers saved. If possible, keep a spare phone or SIM card hidden.

🏠

Identify a safe place

Know where you can go at short notice: a relative, friend, shelter, or place of worship.

🗣️

Agree on a code word

Pick a word or phrase to signal a trusted person that you need help, without the abuser knowing.

🧒

Plan for your children

Teach them how to call 112. Identify a safe neighbor they can run to. Reassure them it is not their job to protect you.

Know your rights

The law is on your side

Nigeria has laws that protect survivors of domestic violence. You have the right to be safe.

🇳🇬 Nigerian Law

Violence Against Persons (Prohibition) Act, 2015 (VAPP Act)

This federal law criminalizes all forms of violence: physical, sexual, psychological, and economic abuse within relationships. It provides for protection orders, compensation for survivors, and penalties of up to life imprisonment for offenders.

Key protections under the Act:

• You can apply for a protection order at any High Court to stop the abuser from contacting or approaching you.
• The law covers all relationships: marriage, cohabitation, dating, and family.
• Emotional and economic abuse are recognized as criminal offenses, not just physical violence.
• Reporting can be done to the police, NAPTIP, or the Ministry of Women Affairs.

Additionally, the Child Rights Act (2003) protects children from all forms of abuse, and many states have domestic violence response teams (like Lagos DSVRT) that provide immediate support.

The unseen victims

How abuse affects children

Children who witness domestic violence are deeply affected, even if they are never physically harmed. They see, hear, and feel more than adults realize.

😰

Emotional impact

Anxiety, nightmares, bedwetting, withdrawal, or aggression. Children often blame themselves.

📉

Development

Difficulty concentrating in school, regression in milestones, and trouble forming healthy relationships later.

🔄

Cycle risk

Without support, children who grow up in abusive homes are more likely to experience or perpetrate abuse as adults.

💚

Hope and healing

With safety, counseling, and a stable caregiver, children can and do recover fully. Early support makes a lasting difference.
